# Day 7: Sanding & Surface Prep

## SESSION OVERVIEW

The frame is structurally complete. Now we prepare the surface for finishing. Sanding is unglamorous and repetitive. It is also the foundation of every coat that goes on afterwards. A poorly sanded frame looks poor no matter how good the paint is.

## Key Terms

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Grit | The size of the abrasive particles on sandpaper. Low numbers (80) are rough. High numbers (240) are fine and smooth |
| Key | A slightly roughened surface that helps paint stick. If a surface is too smooth, paint can peel off |
| Tack cloth | A slightly sticky cloth used to remove all dust from a surface before painting |
| Grit sequence | Sanding from rough to fine in steps. Each grit removes the scratches from the previous one |
| Primer | The first coat applied to a surface. It seals the bamboo and gives the topcoat something to bond to |
| Grain direction | Sanding along the length of the bamboo fibres, not across them. Sanding across the grain raises the fibres |

## TEACHING SEQUENCE

### Block 1 | Surface Prep Briefing & Grit Demo (9:30–10:00, 30 min)

**What To Do**

- Show the grit sequence on the board: 80 > 120 > 180 > 240. Explain what each does.

- Demo on scrap bamboo: sand one section with only 240 (result: looks smooth but scratches from the rough bamboo show through). Sand another section through all 4 grits (result: genuinely smooth).

- Ask learners: “Why can't we just use 240 straight off?” Guide them to: the surface has irregularities 240 cannot remove quickly; you need coarser paper first.

**Example of What To Say**

- “The primer will follow every mark you leave. Scratches from 80-grit will show through the paint unless you remove them with 120. Every grit removes the marks from the last one.”

**SEND Adaptation Tips**

- Reference card showing grit sequence with visual description of what each step achieves

- Allow learners to touch each grit before starting so they can feel the difference

### Block 2 | 80-Grit Sanding (10:00–11:00, 60 min)

**What To Do**

- Learners sand their whole frame with 80-grit. Goal: remove epoxy ridges at joint lines, rough bamboo surface, any irregularities.

- Technique: wrap sandpaper round sanding block for flat areas. Use folded paper for curved surfaces and joint zones.

- Sand along the grain of the bamboo — not across it.

- Do not sand through the outer layer of the bamboo. Stop if you see colour change in the bamboo fibre.

**What To Look For**

- Sanding too aggressively on the bamboo outer layer — redirect to joint zones which need more work

- Learners skipping the joint zones — these are the areas that need the most work

**SEND Adaptation Tips**

- Grip difficulties: bench vice to hold frame, both hands on sanding block

- More frequent rest breaks within the block for learners with fatigue

### Block 3 | 120 and 180-Grit (11:15–12:30, 75 min)

**What To Do**

- Before moving to 120, brush/wipe dust from frame and do a hand-feel check. Surface should feel noticeably smoother than raw bamboo.

- 120-grit: follow the same technique. Goal is to remove the scratches left by 80-grit.

- Instructor mid-check at 45 minutes: run hand over each frame, look for any remaining 80-grit scratches.

- 180-grit: same again. Surface should now feel genuinely smooth to the back of the hand.

**Example of What To Say**

- “Look at the surface in a raking light — hold it at an angle to the light. Scratches show up clearly. If you can see scratches, the next coat of paint will show them too.”

### Block 4 | 240-Grit & Tack Cloth (1:30–2:30, 60 min)

**What To Do**

- 240-grit: light passes over the whole frame. This is the finishing grit — not for material removal.

- Wipe the whole frame with tack cloth. Use a fresh cloth section for each major area.

- Instructor surface check: run back of hand over whole frame. Feel for any rough spots. Check in raking light for visible scratches.

- Sign off each frame as “Ready to prime” in workbook. Any frames not ready get additional 180-grit before sign-off.

**Example of What To Say**

- “The tack cloth picks up everything the brush leaves behind. If you skip it, dust gets locked under your primer. That shows in the final finish.”

### Block 5 | Workbook & Oral Q&A (2:30–3:00, 30 min)

**Oral Q&A Questions**

- “Why do we sand through a sequence of grits instead of just using the finest one?”

- “What does keying a surface mean, and why is it important?”

- “Why is tack cloth used instead of a dry cloth?”

- “What would happen if you painted straight onto unsanded bamboo?”

## Differentiation

#### FOR LOW LITERACY

The sanding sequence is physical — teach by demonstration, not reading. The grit numbers on the sandpaper are the only text learners need to navigate. A simple laminated card showing "80 → 120 → 240" is sufficient reference material.

#### FOR ADHD

Sanding is repetitive and absorbing — often a good session for ADHD learners. The natural task progression (one grade, move to next) provides structure. Use the grade change as a group checkpoint: everyone moves to 120-grit together at the same time.

#### FOR ANXIOUS LEARNERS

The tactile, self-paced nature of sanding is calming. There is no way to "fail" at sanding — only to take longer. Reassure: "There's no rush. When it feels smooth, you're done with that section."

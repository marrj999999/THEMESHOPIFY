# Day 1: Design Brief & Child Sizing

## SESSION OVERVIEW

Today learners meet the programme and meet the challenge. They take real measurements for a real child and use those measurements to start their design brief. Everything built over the next 10 days begins here.

## Key Terms

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Design brief | A written plan that says what you are building, who it is for, and what sizes it must be |
| Specification | The exact measurements and materials needed to build something correctly |
| Seat height | How high the seat is from the ground — must match the child's inside leg length |
| Inseam | The inside leg measurement — from crotch to floor. Used to set seat height on a balance bike |
| Scale drawing | A drawing where all the measurements are shown to a set scale — so the drawing shows the real shape accurately |
| Tube diameter | How wide a tube is — measured across the middle. Different parts of the frame use different sizes |
| BS EN 14765 | The British and European safety standard for children's bicycles. The bike must meet this to be safe |

## TEACHING SEQUENCE

### Block 1 | Welcome Back & Programme Introduction (9:30–10:00, 30 min)

**What You'll Need**

- Example balance bike on the bench

- Programme overview sheet (or whiteboard)

- Workbooks — do not hand out yet

**What To Do**

- Greet each learner by name. Remind them of workshop rules from their Level 2 course.

- Show the finished example bike. Pass it around. Let them hold it.

- Say: “Over the next 10 days, you will each build one of these. The bike you build is for a specific child — someone you have in mind. Everything we measure, everything we cut, is for that child.”

- Outline the 10-day plan on the whiteboard. Highlight Day 5 (bonding), Day 9 (safety test), Day 10 (handover).

**Example of What To Say**

- “You already know how to work bamboo. This course is about putting that skill to use for someone who matters to you.”

- “The bike has to be safe. Not just finished — safe. We'll use the same standard they use for bikes sold in shops.”

**What To Look For**

- Learners engaging with the example bike — this usually sparks questions. Let them run.

- Anyone quiet or disengaged — check in quietly before Block 2

**SEND Adaptation Tips**

- Learners with low confidence in reading: pair with workbook — go through orally first

- Learners with limited English: use the bamboo sample display to explain sizes visually

- Learners with motor difficulties: note now so jig and tool adaptations can be planned ahead

### Block 2 | Design Brief & Child Sizing (10:00–11:00, 60 min)

**What You'll Need**

- Workbooks — open to design brief pages

- Child sizing chart — 1 per learner

- Measuring tapes and rules

**What To Do**

- Hand out workbooks. Ask each learner to write the name or initials of the child they are building for on the cover.

- Explain seat height: “A balance bike seat must sit just below the child's hip. Both feet flat on the floor. That's how they learn to balance.”

- Use sizing chart together. Walk through how to read inseam measurement against age range.

- Each learner fills in: target child age, estimated inseam, required seat height range, handlebar height, frame length.

- Circulate. Check every measurement is recorded correctly before moving on.

**Example of What To Say**

- “If you don't know their exact measurements, use the age range on the chart. We can adjust later if you find out.”

- “This is the most important page in the workbook. Everything else is built on these numbers.”

**What To Look For**

- Incorrect readings from the sizing chart — common mistake is using height instead of inseam

- Learners who seem uncertain about who they are building for — handle sensitively, they can use a generic child profile

**SEND Adaptation Tips**

- Low literacy: scribe for learner or use verbal confirmation with instructor writing

- Dyscalculia: use pre-numbered chart with tick boxes rather than blank measurement fields

- Vision impairment: use large-print sizing chart variant

### Block 3 | Bamboo Specification Planning (11:15–12:00, 45 min)

**What You'll Need**

- Bamboo tube samples — full display set

- Bamboo specification reference sheet

- Workbooks — specification table page

**What To Do**

- Pass around the bamboo tube samples. Ask learners to recall from Level 2: which diameter for which part?

- Explain: “Head tube and seat tube take the most load. Down tube is the spine. Chainstays are thinner — they just hold the wheel in place.”

- Using the child measurements from Block 2, learners select the correct tube sizes and cut lengths for each part of their frame.

- Record in the bamboo specification table: component name, tube diameter, wall thickness, cut length.

**Example of What To Say**

- “You're choosing these sizes based on what will keep that child safe. Too thin and it can flex. Too heavy and the bike is hard to lift.”

- “Write it down exactly. This table is your shopping list for tomorrow when we cut.”

**What To Look For**

- Learners choosing tubes by appearance rather than specification — redirect to the chart

- Specification table left incomplete — all fields must be filled before Day 2

**SEND Adaptation Tips**

- Learners with memory difficulties: laminated reference card with tube sizes and frame components

- Learners who struggle with tables: allow annotated drawing instead of table

### Block 4 | Design Drawing (12:00–12:30, 30 min)

**What You'll Need**

- Workbooks — drawing page

- Pencils and rules

- Example sketch on whiteboard (pre-drawn by instructor)

**What To Do**

- Draw a simple side-elevation example on the whiteboard: head tube, down tube, seat tube, chainstays, fork. Label each with measurement.

- Learners copy the structure and add their own measurements.

- No artistic marks needed — straight lines and clear numbers only.

- Instructor initials each completed drawing before lunch.

**Example of What To Say**

- “This isn't an art class. It's a plan. If the measurements are right and the lines are roughly in the right place, it's a good drawing.”

**What To Look For**

- Learners spending too long on artistic detail — refocus on accuracy

- Measurements missing or not matching their specification table — must be consistent

**SEND Adaptation Tips**

- Fine motor difficulties: pre-printed frame outline with blank measurement boxes to fill in

- Dyslexia: allow verbal description of drawing with instructor noting key measurements

### Block 5 | Workbook Review & Oral Q&A (1:30–2:30, 60 min)

**What You'll Need**

- Completed workbook pages from morning

- Instructor observation record sheet

**What To Do**

- Sit with each learner in turn. Review their design brief, specification table, and drawing.

- Ask 2–3 oral questions per learner (see below). Record answers and sign off in observation notes.

- Learners not with instructor continue correcting or adding detail to their workbook.

**Oral Q&A Questions (choose 2–3 per learner)**

- “Why did you choose this seat height?”

- “What would happen if the seat was too high for the child?”

- “Why does the head tube need to be a wider diameter than the chainstays?”

- “What safety standard does the finished bike need to meet?”

- “If you got the child's measurements wrong, when would you find out?”

**What To Look For**

- Learners who cannot justify their measurement choices — they need more guided support

- Strong answers — note these in the observation record as positive evidence

**SEND Adaptation Tips**

- Non-verbal learners: accept written or drawn responses

- Anxiety around questioning: frame as “a chat about what you did” not a test

### Block 6 | Day Close & Preview (2:30–3:00, 30 min)

**What To Do**

- Brief group: what did we decide today? Ask each learner to name one measurement they recorded.

- Preview Day 2: “Tomorrow we go into the bamboo store and select your actual tubes. Your specification sheet is your guide.”

- Collect workbooks — store securely.

- Tool count and shadow board check. Sign off with supervising officer.

## Differentiation

#### FOR LOW LITERACY (assumed 65% of cohort)

Every chart and template must work without reading: use diagrams, numbers, and physical samples as primary communicators. Read every prompt aloud before asking learners to write. Oral responses are valid for all assessment criteria — scribe verbatim and note "recorded verbally, scribed by instructor." Pair written tasks with a physical anchor: "Look at the bike. Now draw the top tube on your sheet."

#### FOR ADHD (assume undiagnosed in ~25% of cohort)

Break tasks into single steps. Give one instruction at a time. Use physical movement between tasks — "pick up the tape measure, let's measure the saddle height on the demo bike." Frequent check-ins: "How far have you got? Show me." Keep activities hands-on where possible.

#### FOR RESISTANT LEARNERS

Don't oversell the emotional angle. Lead with the craft: "You're building a bike. The job is to get the sizes right." Buy-in typically comes Days 3–4 when the frame starts to look like something. Don't push today.

#### FOR EMOTIONAL REACTIONS

Thinking about their child may be difficult for some learners. Do not draw attention to this. If a learner becomes quiet or steps back, let them. Do not probe. The work itself is grounding — keep the practical tasks moving.

# UCL engineering students designed a bamboo cargo trike for Ethiopian farmers

## Context

Across three consecutive years — 2016, 2017 and 2018 — Master's students in UCL's
Department of Mechanical Engineering ran live engineering design projects with Bamboo
Bicycle Club, each cohort building on the last. Real briefs, real prototypes, supervised
within the department.

## What we did

We set students a genuine design-for-development challenge: build a load-carrying bamboo
bike that could work in a low-resource setting. One project, "Pedal Progress" — supervised
by Dr Eral Bele — focused on Ethiopia, chosen for its large agricultural sector and its
bamboo forests. The brief was to fill the gap between carrying goods by hand and using a
motorised vehicle: a cargo bike that is durable, simple to build, affordable, and low in
environmental impact.

## Verified outcome

The Pedal Progress prototype was a rear-loading bamboo tricycle with:

- a **150kg** cargo capacity
- **hemp-resin joints**
- **seven-speed gearing with a rear differential**
- an expected lifespan of around **five years**
- a lower carbon footprint than an equivalent steel bike

A later UCL cohort developed an electric version, adding an integrated battery, a rear-hub
motor and a weatherproof cargo box.

## Quote

No participant quote is verified for this project, so we make none up. The work speaks for
itself: a working prototype, designed and tested by Master's engineers against a real
Ethiopian farming brief.

## Why it matters

It is one of the clearest examples of Bamboo Bicycle Club as a design-for-development
partner, not just a bike kit. Real students, a real country brief, and a real prototype —
bamboo applied to a genuine mobility problem.

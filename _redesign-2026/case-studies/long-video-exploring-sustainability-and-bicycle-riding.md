# Sustainability in motion: Kate Rawles and The Life Cycle

> `/blogs/impact/kate-rawles-life-cycle-8288-miles-bamboo`

Environmental writer and cyclist **Kate Rawles** built her own bamboo bicycle at the Bamboo Bicycle Club in London — her first ever bike build — and then rode it 8,288 miles down the spine of the Andes, from the Caribbean coast of Colombia to Ushuaia at the southern tip of Argentina.

She named the bike **Woody**.

## A bike built for the story it would tell

Woody was, in Kate's own words, "probably the UK's first home grown bicycle." The bamboo was grown at the Eden Project in Cornwall; the joints were made from Yorkshire hemp soaked in a UK-made plant-based resin. For a journey about biodiversity and the systems that sustain life, the material mattered as much as the miles.

## The Life Cycle

The expedition took Kate through Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia and Argentina over roughly thirteen months, investigating why biodiversity is collapsing — and what people are doing to turn it around. She saw ecosystems under pressure, and she met communities working to regenerate them.

> "The Life Cycle is about biodiversity. It's not just a cycling trip — it's an attempt to show that you can travel in a low-carbon way while also raising awareness of one of the most important but under-reported environmental crises of our time." — Kate Rawles

## Why it matters

The journey became a book, *The Life Cycle: 8,000 Miles in the Andes by Bamboo Bike* (Icon Books, 2023), with endorsements from former BBC News Science Editor David Shukman, Eden Project co-founder Sir Tim Smit OBE, and Jonathon Porritt.

For Bamboo Bicycle Club, Kate's ride is the clearest possible proof of the material: a bike built by a first-timer that held together across the Andes, the salt flats and the Patagonian wind. Bamboo isn't a novelty — it is one of the fastest-growing plants on earth, it regrows in three to five years from its root stock without replanting, and a finished BBC frame produces 56.7% less CO2 than an equivalent aluminium frame (independent lifecycle analysis, 2024). Riding it across a continent is, itself, a low-carbon way to travel.
